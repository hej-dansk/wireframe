1. Common Gender: Rules by Meaning

Common gender nouns take en (indefinite) and -(e)n (definite): en hånd, hånden (a hand, the hand). ~75% of Danish nouns are common gender.

RULE
CategoryCommon gender (default)Exceptions → neuter
Peopleen dreng, en far, en kone, en lærer, en svensker, en søster, en udlændinget barn, et bud, et geni, et individ, et medlem, et menneske, et vidne
Animalsen gås, en hund, en kat, en ko, en lakset bæst, et dyr (+ et pattedyr), et egern, et føl, et får, et insekt, et kid, et kreatur, kvæget, et lam, et møl, et svin (+ et marsvin), et æsel
Plants/trees/fruiten birk, en blomst, en eg, en nød, en plante, en pære, en roseet bær, et frø, græsset, et løg, et træ, et æble; any compound in -frø or -træ
Festivals & monthsi julen, i påsken, en kold januar—
Seasonssommeren, vinterenforåret, efteråret (because “år” is neuter)
Lakes & riversen flod, en strøm, en å, Rhinen, den Blå Nil—
Numbers-as-nounsen toer, en tieret ettal, et hundred(e), et tusind(e)
Fractions in -delen fjerdedel, en femtedel—

Pronoun quirk: even when a person-word is grammatically neuter (et barn, et medlem), Danes still use han/hun for a specific person — only det for someone unknown/general. The reverse also holds: common-gender person words (en gæst, en læge) still take han/hun, not den. And a neuter noun used figuratively for a person stays neuter.

With pets: owners often say han/hun; strangers describing someone else's pet often say den. Fairy-tale animals get han/hun.

3. Neuter Gender: Rules by Meaning

Neuter nouns take et (indefinite) and -(e)t (definite): et hus, huset.

RULE
CategoryNeuter (default)Common-gender exceptions
Substancesbrød, glas, guld, jern, kød, papir, snavs, sølv, vandjorden, luften, regnen, sneen, ulden, vinen
Areas/localitieset amt, et distrikt, et kontinent, et land, et område, et sogn, et torven by, en gård, en verden, en ø
Letters of the alphabetet langt u (bogstav understood)—
Nouns from other parts of speechet hvorfor, jeget, et nej—
Collectiveset folk, et følge, et hold, et selskaben forsamling, en gruppe
Languages from adjectivesmit engelsk, det ældste dansk (sprog understood)some also take -en: dansken, tysken

Geographic naming logic: when a country/mountain/ocean name is implied, the phrase agrees neuter (et land, et bjerg, et hav understood): det lille Danmark, det gamle Rom, det store København. But towns/islands lean common (en by, en ø understood), as do seas/lakes/rivers: den smukke Bodensø, den lange Donau, Nilen.

5. Tricky Suffixes & Loanword Gender

RULE

Suffixes like -al, -ar, -el, -ent, -i, -at lean one way but keep real exceptions worth a dictionary check. English loanwords with no built-in gender clue often go common for people/animals (en fan, en kænguru), neuter by pattern-matching (-um → et faktum), or by resemblance to the closest Danish synonym (et team ~ et hold).

6. Compounds, Gendered Pairs & Substance Flexibility

RULE

Compounds take the gender of their last element: en skole + et køkken → et skolekøkken. Male/female pairs (hane/høne, tyr/ko, konge/dronning) are largely fixed vocabulary. Substance nouns can shift gender by context — mostly neuter (brødet, saltet), a few common (cementen, vinen), and a handful genuinely either (gummi, jod, kanel).

7. Plural Formation: The Three Declensions

RULE

1st declension (~60%, -(e)r): mostly vowel-ending and polysyllabic/loanwords — en by → byer. 2nd declension (~25%, -e): most monosyllabic common-gender consonant endings — en dreng → drenge. 3rd declension (~15%, no ending): many monosyllabic neuter nouns — et år → år. Several vowel-changing plurals exist: mand→mænd, bog→bøger, nat→nætter.

8. Plurals of Loanwords

RULE

Many old -s plurals have shifted to native patterns (en baby→babyer), while nouns in -er take -e (computer→computere). Latin loans in -um/-ium/-us/-a either keep a Latin plural (et faktum→fakta) or take a Danish ending (drama→dramaer), and -o words usually just add the Danish ending (video→videoer).

9. Nouns With No Plural, No Singular, and Collectives

RULE

Verbal nouns in -en, most abstract nouns, and substances (guld, kød, vand) are usually singular-only. Two-piece clothing (bukser, jeans), family collectives (forældre, søskende) and a handful of other words (briller, penge) are plural-only. Some words carry both a collective and a countable-double-plural sense: øl (types) vs. øller (individual glasses).

RULE

RULE

12. The Genitive

Danish forms the genitive by adding -s to the base noun (indefinite/definite, singular/plural): en piges, pigens, pigers, pigernes. No apostrophe before the -s: et barns ansigt (a child's face).

RULE
RuleExample
Names ending in -s/-x/-z → apostrophe only, no extra sLars' lejlighed, Marx' bøger, Schweiz' hovedstad
Abbreviations without a full stop → 'sUSA's hær (or Natos, if it reads as a word)
Two-word places ending in -havn/-holm/-land → +sKøbenhavns Slot
Adjectival nouns (adjective-as-noun) → +sden gamles sparepenge

⚠️ Golden rule: a noun following a genitive never gets its own definite article. Bakkens top (never "bakkens toppen"). Pigernes forældre (never "pigernes forældrene").

13. The Indefinite Article

en = common gender, et = neuter. No separate plural form — plain plurals need no article: piger (girls). Note: en also means "one" — context tells you which job it's doing.

14. The Definite Article

Danish glues the article onto the noun's end instead of using a separate word: -(e)n for singular common gender, -(e)t for singular neuter. The moment an adjective steps in front, the end-article drops and a separate front-article appears instead: den (common sg.), det (neuter sg.), de (any plural).

RULE
PatternRuleExample
Unstressed -e endingadd just -n/-ten uge→ugen; et billede→billedet
Stressed -e endingfull -enen café→caféen; en idé→idéen
Latin -eum/-iumdrop -ummuseum→museet; studium→studiet
Short stressed voweldouble final consonantbus→bussen; hat→hatten; hotel→hotellet

15. Choosing Between Indefinite, Definite, and No Article

Danish plays with three states: no article, indefinite, definite — and switching changes meaning. The general rule matches English (known → definite, new → indefinite), but Danish leans on shared context more, so it often diverges — using the definite article where English uses none (livet i det moderne samfund), dropping the article where English keeps "the" (næste dag), and dropping the indefinite article for nationality/profession/religion (Marie er dansker) unless a qualifier or figurative sense returns it (Han er en dygtig læge).

What the Exam Actually Tests

PD2 focus: everyday mechanics without hesitation — en/et for common nouns, the three plural declensions, definite-article endings, and the basic -s genitive (no English-style apostrophe). This is exactly what Delprøve 2B tests, and what an examiner tracks as grammatisk færdighed while you describe a picture or answer aloud. Goal: produce en bil, bilen, biler, bilerne, bilens without pausing.

PD3 focus: a sharper eye for exceptions and nuance — gender-by-suffix rules and their exceptions on unfamiliar words, trickier loanword/Latin plurals from B2 reading texts, the apostrophe rule for names in -s/-x/-z, genitive of measurement and colourful genitive idioms, and finer indefinite/definite/no-article choices, including noun homonyms that could throw off reading comprehension.
